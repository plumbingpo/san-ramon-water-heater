What makes a water heater installation different from a replacement? Mostly, the new work. Adding a heater for an addition, relocating one out of a living space, upgrading to a larger tank or a tankless unit, or converting between gas, electric, and heat pump all require piping, venting, or wiring that did not exist before. In San Ramon, CA, that work also has to meet local code, which can mean permits, drain pans, expansion tanks, and seismic straps depending on the location. We plan every piece up front, so the heater performs, the work passes, and nothing is improvised on install day.

Good installations start on paper. We size tanks by first-hour rating and tankless units by gallons per minute at the temperature rise your incoming water needs, so the heater keeps up with your busiest hour. Then we look at where it will go. A gas unit needs a vent path to the outside and enough combustion air. A heat pump model needs space, a room that stays reasonably warm, and a condensate drain. Electric tankless units can need a heavy dedicated circuit and the panel capacity behind it. Above finished space or in an attic, the heater needs a drain pan piped somewhere safe. We work through all of it with you in plain language, then give you a firm price.

The install itself follows a checklist: gas piping sized, run, fitted with a sediment trap, and leak-tested, vents built to the manufacturer's size and pitch, circuits and breakers matched to the heater's load, and water lines connected with shutoffs and dielectric protection between copper and steel. A full-size relief valve discharge line runs to a safe point, and any required expansion tank, drain pan, seismic straps, or permit is part of the job. Before we leave, we fill and purge the tank, check draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, set the temperature near 120 degrees, and walk you through everything. Why Professional Water Heater Installation Matters

Consider the gas side first. A new heater on an undersized line starves for fuel whenever the furnace or range is running, and a whole-house tankless unit often needs a larger line than the house already has. Vent material, size, pitch, and clearances all have to be right, or exhaust drifts back inside. Electric models need their own math on breaker, wire gauge, and panel capacity. Add relief valve piping, dielectric fittings, expansion tanks, drain pans, seismic straps, and permit rules that vary from one jurisdiction to the next, and there are a lot of details to get right.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Here are our recommendations:

  • Store paint, gasoline, and solvents far from any gas water heater
  • Glance at the pan and discharge pipe every month or two; water there means it is time to call
  • Keep the temperature around 120 degrees; hotter settings raise the risk of scalding
  • Leave working space around the heater, and never stack storage against it
  • Drain a few gallons from a new tank twice a year to control sediment
  • Tankless heaters need a yearly flush to clear scale from the heat exchanger
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years to protect the tank
